01 / TIME & CYCLES

The Calendar of the Hebrew Bible

A lunar year of 354 days, forced to keep step with the farming seasons. Months with no names, two new years, and festivals that mostly fall exactly on a new moon or a full one.

The outer ring is the phase of the moon on each of the 354 days of the year — twelve cycles of waxing and waning. Select a festival marker to read about it.

How a month was fixed

The lunar cycle runs twenty-nine and a half days. Since a day cannot be split, a month is either 29 or 30 — and the decision was made by eye, on sighting the first crescent. That is why Saul's "tomorrow is the new moon" ran to two days: nobody knew in advance which of them it would be.

Notice what falls where: the Day of Acclamation on the first of the month — new moon, an invisible disc. Passover and Tabernacles on the fifteenth — full moon, a lit night for pilgrims. Psalm 81 puts both in one breath: "Blow the horn at the new moon, at the full moon for our feast day."

The month names

In the Torah the months have no names at all — only numbers. Four Canaanite names survive in the book of Kings. The names in use today are Babylonian, and they entered only after the exile — and even then, only seven of the twelve appear in the Hebrew Bible at all.

What is disputed

The biblical calendar is not one consistent document. Points where the texts disagree with each other, or say nothing at all.

"On the day after the sabbath" — when the count begins

The waving of the omer is set "on the day after the sabbath". If "the sabbath" is the first festival day of Passover, the count begins on the sixteenth of Nisan and Weeks always falls on the sixth of Sivan — this was the practice of the Pharisees and the ruling that became law. If "the sabbath" is an ordinary Saturday, the count always begins on a Sunday and Weeks always falls on a Sunday — this was the reading of the Boethusians, and it is what Karaites and Samaritans follow to this day. The verse itself does not settle it.

Leviticus 23:11–16

The seventh of Av, or the tenth

2 Kings dates the burning of the Temple to the seventh of the fifth month. Jeremiah, describing that same event, dates it to the tenth. The two dates cannot both stand, and neither of them is the Ninth of Av that was later fixed.

2 Kings 25:8 · Jeremiah 52:12

Ezekiel's festival calendar

The vision of the future Temple carries a different festival calendar: purification of the sanctuary on the first of Nisan and again on the seventh, Passover on the fourteenth, and a seven-day festival on the fifteenth of the seventh month. It has no Day of Atonement, no Weeks, and no Eighth Day of Assembly. The rabbis wrestled with the discrepancy, and tradition holds that they considered suppressing the book because of it.

Ezekiel 45:18–25

Two new years

Nisan is "the first of months", and the festivals and regnal years are counted by it. But the farming year closes with the ingathering in the seventh month — "at the going out of the year", "at the turn of the year" — and that same month holds the Day of Acclamation, the Day of Atonement and the opening of the jubilee year. The two systems run in parallel throughout the Hebrew Bible.

Exodus 12:2 · Exodus 23:16 · Exodus 34:22 · Leviticus 25:9

And what is not in the Hebrew Bible at all

Hanukkah appears in no book of the Hebrew Bible — it comes from the books of Maccabees, which did not enter the canon. Nor are Tu BiShvat, Lag BaOmer or the Seventeenth of Tammuz as a date. Purim is the exception — the only festival instituted inside the Hebrew Bible itself, in the book of Esther, and not at Sinai.

Esther 9:20–32

Notes

  • The synodic month is 29.53 days, so a year of twelve months is about 354 days — some 11 days shorter than the solar year. The Torah requires Passover to fall in "the month of Aviv", which means intercalation is necessary, yet the Hebrew Bible describes no mechanism for deciding it. The fixed nineteen-year intercalation cycle is Babylonian in origin and was settled far later.
  • The farming ring is based on the Gezer Calendar, a Hebrew inscription from the tenth century BCE — an archaeological source, not a biblical one. Lining it up with the calendar months is a commonly accepted scholarly reconstruction, not something the inscription states.
  • The date of the Fast of Gedaliah does not appear in the text — Jeremiah says only "in the seventh month". Its position on the wheel follows the later tradition.
  • The biblical day begins at evening, as the Day of Atonement command spells out: "From evening to evening you shall keep your sabbath."
