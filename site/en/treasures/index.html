02 / PLACE & MOVEMENT

The Treasures

Wealth left the Temple and the royal treasury again and again — sometimes as plunder, sometimes as a bribe paid willingly. The Hebrew Bible records most of it with an accountant's precision: who, when, how much, and where to. And a few items simply drop out of the record and are never mentioned again.

The balance

What left and what came back, according to the record itself.

Went out

  • Five hundred gold shields Solomon had made — to Shishak
  • The treasures of the House of the Lord and of the royal palace — over and over, in nine separate events
  • The gold stripped from the doors and doorposts of the sanctuary — to Sennacherib
  • The gold vessels Solomon made for the sanctuary — cut apart by Nebuchadnezzar in 597
  • The bronze pillars Jachin and Boaz, the bronze Sea and its twelve oxen, and the stands — smashed to pieces in 586 and the bronze carried off

Came back

  • 5,400 gold and silver vessels under the edict of Cyrus, counted out to Sheshbazzar
  • 650 talents of silver, 100 talents of gold and further vessels brought by Ezra, weighed out into the hands of twelve priests
  • The returnees' donation to the treasury: 61,000 gold drachmas, 5,000 minas of silver, 100 priestly garments
And what appears on no return list at all: the bronze. The two pillars, the Sea, the stands and the twelve oxen were not carried off intact — they were broken up on the spot and the bronze loaded as raw metal. "The bronze of all these vessels was beyond weighing." Broken metal is not a vessel that can be handed back by count, which is why it is missing from the list of Cyrus. The Ark does not appear either — not on the list of what was taken, and not on the list of what was returned.

Notes

  • A talent is about three thousand shekels. Converting it to kilograms depends on the weight standard, which varied between periods and between kingdoms; the usual estimates run around thirty to thirty-six kilograms per talent. Every conversion here is an order of magnitude only.
  • Dates follow the chronology accepted in scholarship; some of them carry a margin of a year or two.
  • The parallels in Chronicles add details absent from Kings, and sometimes word things differently. Where that happens both sources are cited.
  • The identifications of Ebenezer, of the house of Obed-edom and of the location of Gath are disputed, and are marked on the map accordingly.
  • The extra-biblical sources cited — the Shishak relief at Karnak and the Sennacherib Prism — are brought only where they record the same event, and are not part of the Hebrew Bible.
